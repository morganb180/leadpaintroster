Lead clearance testing after abatement

Last updated 2026-10-31. Checked against the official sources listed at the end of this page. Clearance rules differ by program, so confirm which rules apply to your job.

Reviewed by Lead Paint Roster staff

Clearance testing is the final check after lead abatement or lead hazard control work. A certified inspector or risk assessor looks for leftover dust and paint chips, takes dust wipe samples from floors, window sills and window troughs, and sends them to an accredited lab. Since January 12, 2026, EPA's post-abatement clearance levels are 5, 40 and 100 µg/ft².

At a glance

  • Steps: visual assessment, dust wipe samples, lab analysis, report (24 CFR 35.1340).
  • EPA clearance (dust-lead action) levels since January 12, 2026: floors 5, window sills 40, window troughs 100 µg/ft² (Federal Register).
  • Previous levels were 10, 100 and 400 µg/ft² (EPA).
  • After abatement, clearance must be done by a certified inspector or risk assessor.
  • The tester must be independent of whoever did the work.
  • Failed areas are recleaned and retested until they pass.

What clearance testing is for

Abatement and repair work can leave fine lead dust behind, even when surfaces look clean. EPA uses dust-lead action levels to decide when an abatement is complete: after abatement, testing must show dust-lead levels below those levels before the job can be considered done (EPA).

The steps of a clearance exam

HUD's rule for federally assisted housing lays out the steps, following EPA's abatement rules at 40 CFR 745.227(e) (24 CFR 35.1340):

  1. Visual assessment. The examiner checks for deteriorated paint and visible dust, debris or paint chips. Anything found must be cleaned up before sampling continues. If outside paint was disturbed, the ground and outdoor living areas are checked too.
  2. Dust wipe sampling. Wipe samples are taken from floors and, where practicable, interior window sills and window troughs. Soil sampling is not required.
  3. Lab analysis. Samples go to an EPA-recognized laboratory.
  4. Report. The report lists dates, the examiner and certification number, visual results, dust results by location in µg/ft², and the lab.

For more than 10 similar units in a multifamily property, random sampling may be allowed. If containment kept dust inside the work area, sometimes only the worksite needs clearance.

Current EPA clearance levels

EPA's 2024 rule lowered the post-abatement clearance levels, now called dust-lead action levels. They apply from the compliance date of January 12, 2026 (Federal Register):

SurfaceBefore Jan 12, 2026From Jan 12, 2026
Floors10 µg/ft²5 µg/ft²
Window sills100 µg/ft²40 µg/ft²
Window troughs400 µg/ft²100 µg/ft²

The same rule set dust-lead reportable levels at any reportable level measured by an accredited lab. If clearance results are below the action levels but at or above reportable levels, the abatement report must include a statement saying so, and EPA recommends best practices such as HEPA vacuuming and regular damp cleaning (EPA). A 2025 correction restored some post-abatement rule text (Federal Register).

Who can do clearance testing?

  • After abatement: a certified risk assessor or certified lead-based paint inspector (24 CFR 35.1340).
  • After interim controls or other hazard reduction (HUD-covered housing): a certified risk assessor or inspector, a trained sampling technician whose report a certified risk assessor or inspector signs, or a licensed clearance technician for single units.

Clearance must be done by someone independent of those who did the work. In-house staff can do it, but never on their own work (24 CFR 35.1340).

If clearance fails

Every surface represented by a failed sample must be recleaned or treated again and retested until it passes (24 CFR 35.1340). Ask your contractor upfront who pays for recleaning and retesting.

Clearance in NJ, NYC and MA

  • New Jersey: after interim controls in a rental, a dust wipe re-inspection leads to a 2-year lead-safe certificate. After abatement, a lead abatement clearance certificate leads to a lead-free certification (N.J.A.C. 5:28A). See lead-safe vs lead-free.
  • New York City: after work under Local Law 1, dust sampling must be done by an EPA-certified inspector or risk assessor independent of the owner and contractor (NYC HPD). See NYC Local Law 1.
  • Massachusetts: a licensed lead inspector reinspects after deleading before a Letter of Full Deleading Compliance is issued, and a risk assessor takes dust samples for interim control (Mass.gov, Mass.gov).

Frequently asked questions

What are the lead dust clearance levels in 2026?

EPA's post-abatement dust-lead action levels, in effect since January 12, 2026, are 5 µg/ft² for floors, 40 µg/ft² for window sills and 100 µg/ft² for window troughs.

Can my abatement contractor do the clearance test?

No. Clearance must be done by a person or firm independent of those who did the work.

Is soil tested during clearance?

Not under HUD's clearance rule. The examiner does check the ground near disturbed exterior paint and removes visible paint chips.

What happens if the clearance test fails?

The failed areas are recleaned or treated again and retested until results are below the clearance levels.
