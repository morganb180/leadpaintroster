RRP vs lead abatement: what is the difference?

Last updated 2026-10-31. Checked against the official sources listed at the end of this page. General information, not legal advice. Rules change, so confirm details with the agency named.

Reviewed by Lead Paint Roster staff

Lead abatement is work designed to permanently eliminate lead-based paint hazards, often ordered by a government agency. RRP is ordinary renovation, repair or painting in a pre-1978 home that happens to disturb paint. They are separate EPA programs with different certifications: an RRP-certified renovator is not licensed to do abatement.

At a glance

  • Abatement: permanently addresses lead hazards, by removal, sealing or enclosure (EPA).
  • RRP: renovation that disturbs paint in pre-1978 homes, childcare facilities and preschools. Not designed to address lead hazards (EPA).
  • Both require certified individuals and certified firms, under different certifications.
  • Abatement: occupants must be out. RRP: occupants stay out of the work area, and the firm hands out the Renovate Right pamphlet.
  • EPA firm certification fees: $300 renovation, $550 lead-based paint activities or combined. Good for 5 years (EPA).

What is lead abatement?

EPA calls abatement an activity designed to permanently address lead-based paint hazards. Methods include removing, sealing or enclosing lead-based paint with special materials. Painting over it with regular paint is not abatement (EPA). Abatement often starts with an inspection or risk assessment (see inspection vs risk assessment) and ends with clearance testing.

Individuals and firms doing inspections, risk assessments and abatement in pre-1978 housing and child-occupied facilities must be certified and follow specific work practices. EPA runs this program directly in some states, including New York. Other states, such as New Jersey and Massachusetts, run their own EPA-authorized programs (EPA).

What is RRP?

EPA's RRP Rule requires anyone paid to do work that disturbs painted surfaces in homes, childcare facilities and preschools built before 1978 to be certified, with employees trained in lead-safe work practices. This includes all firms, even sole proprietors, plus painters, plumbers, carpenters, electricians and property managers (EPA, EPA).

The rule does not cover (EPA):

  • Housing built in 1978 or later.
  • Housing for elderly or disabled people, or zero-bedroom units, unless a child under 6 lives or is expected to live there.
  • Housing or components declared lead-free by a certified inspector or risk assessor.
  • Minor repairs disturbing 6 square feet or less of paint per room inside, or 20 square feet or less outside. Window replacement and demolition are always covered.

Homeowners working on their own home are generally not covered, but the rule does apply if you rent out all or part of your home, run a childcare center there, or flip houses (EPA). Paint testing is not required, but without documentation that the paint is not lead-based, RRP rules apply.

RRP firm certification and fees

EPA firm certification fees (EPA):

  • Renovation firm: $300.
  • Lead-based paint activities (abatement, inspection, risk assessment): $550.
  • Combined renovation and lead-based paint activities: $550.
  • Tribal firms: $20. Replacement certificate: $15.

Certification is good for 5 years. Apply to recertify at least 90 days before it expires. These are EPA's fees; states that run their own programs set their own.

Which one does my job need?

  • Ordinary renovation or repainting in a pre-1978 home: an EPA lead-safe certified (RRP) firm.
  • A government order, a lead-poisoned child, or you want lead hazards permanently gone: a certified abatement firm.
  • Not sure what is there: start with an inspection or risk assessment.

Some jobs need both certifications (NYC HPD).

State and city rules

  • New Jersey: DCA certifies lead evaluation and abatement firms, and the NJ Department of Health licenses individual workers. Interim controls that are not renovation work need no specific certification (NJ DCA). See NJ's rental lead law.
  • New York City: work done because of violations or city orders requires an EPA-certified abatement firm, and dust sampling afterward by an independent certified inspector or risk assessor (NYC HPD). See Local Law 1.
  • Massachusetts: deleading must be done by a trained and licensed person, though owners can do some tasks after training (Mass.gov). See the Massachusetts Lead Law.

Frequently asked questions

Can an RRP-certified contractor do lead abatement?

No. EPA treats abatement and RRP as separate programs with different certifications. Abatement requires certified abatement workers and supervisors and a certified lead-based paint activities firm.

Does the RRP rule apply to DIY work on my own home?

Generally no, unless you rent out all or part of the home, run a childcare center there, or renovate homes to sell for profit.

How much does EPA RRP firm certification cost?

$300 for a renovation firm, and $550 for lead-based paint activities or combined certification. It lasts 5 years.

Is painting over lead paint abatement?

No. EPA says painting over lead paint hazards with regular paint will not permanently address them.
