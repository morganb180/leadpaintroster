# What is lead paint?

Reviewed by Lead Paint Roster staff

Last updated 2026-10-31. Checked against the official sources listed at the end of this page.

Lead-based paint is paint that contains lead. The federal government banned its sale for use in homes in 1978, and EPA estimates about three-quarters of U.S. homes built before 1978 still contain some. Paint in good condition is usually not a hazard. Peeling, chipping or worn paint, and the dust it makes, is.

At a glance

* Banned for residential use in 1978 ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/reduce-risk-lead-exposure-home)).
* About three-quarters of U.S. homes built before 1978 still contain some lead-based paint ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-abatement-inspection-and-risk-assessment)).
* No safe blood lead level in children has been identified ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/about/index.html)).
* The most common cause of lead exposure in the U.S. is dust from lead-based paint in older buildings ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/learn-about-lead)).
* The only way to know for certain whether a building has lead-based paint is to have it tested ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-abatement-inspection-and-risk-assessment)).

## What counts as lead-based paint?

Lead was used in house paint for decades. Once it is in paint you cannot see it, and lead does not break down over time ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/learn-about-lead)). In 1978 the federal government banned the sale of lead-based paint for use in homes and childcare facilities ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/reduce-risk-lead-exposure-home)).

Whether a coat of paint legally counts as "lead-based" depends on a measured lead level, and each law sets its own threshold. New York City, for example, lowered its threshold to 0.5 milligrams of lead per square centimeter on December 1, 2021 ([NYC HPD](https://www.nyc.gov/site/hpd/services-and-information/paint-testing-requirement-exemptions.page)). A certified inspector measures this with an XRF instrument or paint chip samples.

## Which homes are likely to have it?

Age is the best clue. Homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/reduce-risk-lead-exposure-home)), and the older the home, the more likely. Federal rules treat pre-1978 housing as "target housing" for disclosure and renovation rules.

Much of the housing in the areas we cover is that old. Census data shows 80.1% of homes in Brooklyn (Kings County), 74.2% in Essex County, NJ, and 72.1% in Suffolk County, MA were built before 1980 ([Census ACS 2020-2024](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2024.B25034)). See every county in our [pre-1978 housing by county](https://leadpaintroster.com/pre-1978-housing-by-county/) table.

To check your own building, look at the deed, the original title or your municipal tax records. Ask your landlord if you rent.

## When does lead paint become a hazard?

Lead-based paint in good condition is usually not a hazard. It becomes one when it deteriorates ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-abatement-inspection-and-risk-assessment)):

* **Peeling, chipping, chalking or cracking paint.** Chips and dust can be swallowed or breathed in.
* **Friction and impact surfaces.** Windows, doors and stairs that rub or get bumped grind paint into dust.
* **Renovation.** Sanding, scraping or demolition can quickly create dangerous lead dust ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-abatement-inspection-and-risk-assessment)). That is why paid work in pre-1978 homes must follow EPA's [RRP rule](https://leadpaintroster.com/rrp-vs-lead-abatement/).
* **Soil.** Lead can also be in the soil around a home ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/learn-about-lead)).

## Why lead matters for health

Lead is toxic, and no safe level of lead in children's blood has been identified ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/about/index.html)). Children six and younger are most at risk: their bodies absorb more lead, and they put hands and toys in their mouths ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/learn-about-lead)).

According to EPA, lead exposure in children can cause damage to the brain and nervous system, learning and behavior problems, lower IQ, hearing and speech problems, slowed growth and anemia. In adults it can cause higher blood pressure, kidney problems and fertility problems. In pregnancy, lead stored in the mother's bones can pass to the baby ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/learn-about-lead)).

CDC says even low blood lead levels are linked to developmental delays, difficulty learning and behavior problems. A blood lead test is the best way to know whether a child has been exposed, so talk to your child's healthcare provider ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/about/index.html)).

## How do you find out if your home has lead paint?

Hire a certified professional. There are two main services ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-abatement-inspection-and-risk-assessment)):

* **Lead-based paint inspection:** tells you whether there is lead-based paint and where it is. It does not tell you whether it is a hazard right now.
* **Risk assessment:** tells you whether there are lead hazards from paint, dust or soil, and what to do about them.

Our [inspection vs risk assessment guide](https://leadpaintroster.com/lead-inspection-vs-risk-assessment/) explains which one fits your situation, and the [lead inspection cost guide](https://leadpaintroster.com/lead-inspection-cost/) covers pricing. EPA's Lead-Based Paint Professional Locator and the National Lead Information Center (1-800-424-5323) can help you find certified pros ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-abatement-inspection-and-risk-assessment)).

## How to lower the risk while you live there

EPA recommends these everyday steps in older homes ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/reduce-risk-lead-exposure-home)):

* Check painted surfaces, especially windows, doors and stairs, for peeling or chipping.
* Wet-mop floors and wipe surfaces with a damp cloth. Do not dry sweep.
* If you can see paint chips or debris, use only a HEPA-filter vacuum.
* Clean up chips with a damp paper towel, then wipe the area with a clean wet towel.
* Fix small areas (under 6 square feet) of damaged paint quickly without sanding.
* Before any renovation, hire a lead-safe certified contractor.

## What the law requires in NJ, NY and MA

Federal law requires disclosure when pre-1978 homes are sold or rented (see our [disclosure guide](https://leadpaintroster.com/lead-paint-disclosure/)). Some states and cities go further for rentals:

* **New Jersey:** periodic lead inspections for most pre-1978 rentals. See [NJ's rental lead inspection law](https://leadpaintroster.com/nj-lead-paint-inspection-law/).
* **New York City:** Local Law 1 requires owners to find and fix lead hazards in older buildings. See [NYC Local Law 1](https://leadpaintroster.com/nyc-local-law-1-lead-paint/).
* **Massachusetts:** homes built before 1978 where a child under 6 lives must be deleaded or brought under interim control. See the [Massachusetts Lead Law guide](https://leadpaintroster.com/massachusetts-lead-law/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is lead paint dangerous if it is not peeling?**

Usually not. EPA says lead-based paint in good condition is generally not a hazard. The risk comes from deteriorating paint, friction surfaces like windows and doors, and dust from renovation.

**How can I tell if paint has lead in it?**

You cannot tell by looking. EPA says the only way to know for certain is testing by a certified lead inspector or risk assessor.

**Do homes built after 1978 have lead paint?**

Lead-based paint was banned for residential use in 1978, so newer homes are much less likely to have it. Federal disclosure rules do not apply to housing built after 1977.

**What should I do if my child may have been exposed?**

Talk to your child's healthcare provider about a blood lead test. CDC says a blood lead test is the best way to determine whether a child has been exposed to lead.
